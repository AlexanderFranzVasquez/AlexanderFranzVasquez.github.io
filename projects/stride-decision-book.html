Abstract

Humanoid robots can learn whole-body skills from human motion recorded in everyday video, but single-image 3D body estimators are unreliable on such video. We evaluate SAM 3D Body on NymeriaPlus, daily-life video with a motion-capture ground truth, and propose a prompt corrector: a small network (2.5 M parameters) that keeps SAM 3D Body frozen and predicts the 2D joint positions the model accepts as hints (prompts). On 344 test clips of 6 unseen people, the corrector followed by temporal smoothing reduces the mean joint error from 84.2 to 66.7 mm (−21 %) and the frame-to-frame shaking almost fivefold. A network that corrects the 3D output without seeing the image reaches a similar error (68.7 mm), indicating that most of the gain corrects a systematic bias of the base model. Transferred to a Unitree G1 humanoid, the gain in joint angles is small (16.7° to 16.0°), and in physics simulation, imitation policies trained on either model's motion converge to similar behaviour.

Keywords: human mesh recovery, promptable models, occlusion, motion capture, humanoid imitation, domain shift.

1. Introduction

1.1 Motivation

Imitation learning lets a humanoid robot acquire whole-body skills from human demonstrations, and its bottleneck is data: motion-capture suits and studios are expensive, so the number of demonstrations stays small. Video is the obvious alternative. There are two kinds: cameras installed in a laboratory, with a controlled view of the person, and videos of daily routines, with a moving camera, partial views and occlusion. This project works on the second kind, because it is the one that can scale.

To use such video we need the trajectory of every joint of the body in 3D. Modern single-image models predict the whole body from one picture, but video adds two problems that a single image does not show: the prediction changes from one frame to the next even when the person barely moves, and joints that are hidden (by the body itself, by furniture or by another person) have to be guessed.

1.2 Research question

Can SAM 3D Body, a single-image model, be trusted on everyday video as a source of motion for a humanoid, and what does it take to correct it where it fails? Concretely: which joint trajectories are wrong and why, how much a small learned module can fix without retraining the base model, where that fix comes from, and what of it reaches the robot.

1.3 Approach and contributions

  • A diagnosis of SAM 3D Body (where its 3D joints are wrong, by how much and why) on 4428 daily-life clips of 127 people with a motion-capture ground truth: per joint, over time, under occlusion, and against its own benchmark.
  • The prompt corrector: a small network (2.5 M parameters) that rewrites the 12 joint positions SAM 3D Body accepts as hints (its prompts), without changing the model. It is trained first to place the hints on the true joints in the image, then to make the model output the best 3D body.
  • An analysis of where the gain comes from, with two simpler corrections as baselines: a fixed offset of the prompts and a network that corrects the 3D output without the image.
  • The path to a humanoid: transferring the motion to a Unitree G1 robot on 35 clips (retargeting), and training a controller to imitate one clip in the MuJoCo physics simulator.

1.4 Relation to the course project

The course asks for three phases. The dissection of the base model (Phase A) is Section 4.2. The intervention in the model's core and its controlled ablations (Phase B) are Section 5 and Appendix A. The evaluation under a distribution shift with an analysis of failures (Phase C) is Sections 4.6 and 9: NymeriaPlus is not among the data SAM 3D Body was trained on, and it adds a fisheye camera that moves, distant people and occlusion.

2. Terms and metrics

The body model of this project is MHR (Momentum Human Rig), the skeleton SAM 3D Body predicts and in which NymeriaPlus gives its ground truth. Errors are measured over 18 body joints of MHR (pelvis, hips, knees, ankles, balls of the feet, spine, neck, head, shoulders, elbows, wrists), in the camera frame, averaged over frames with the whole body in view.

2.1 Pose metrics

MPJPE (mm)
Mean per-joint position error after moving the predicted pelvis onto the true pelvis. It measures the pose of the body, not where the body is. The main metric of the report.
PA-MPJPE (mm)
The same after the best rotation, scale and translation (Procrustes alignment): only the shape of the pose, regardless of its orientation and size.
Acceleration error (mm/frame²)
Mean difference between the predicted and the true acceleration of each joint. It measures jitter, the shaking of the joints from frame to frame: a model that shakes from frame to frame has a high value even if its average position is right.
Pelvis error (mm)
Distance between the predicted and the true pelvis in the camera frame. It measures where the body is placed in the scene, mostly its depth.
2D error (px)
Distance in the image between a predicted key point and the true one, on 1024 × 1024 frames.

2.2 Robot metrics

Every motion is retargeted to the Unitree G1 and compared with the ground truth retargeted the same way.

Joint angle error (°)
Mean absolute difference of the robot's 29 joint angles against the ground-truth robot.
Joint jitter (°/frame²)
Mean absolute second difference of the joint angles: how much the robot's joints shake.
Base trajectory error (cm)
Distance between the displacement of the robot's pelvis from the first frame and that of the ground-truth robot.
Foot sliding (cm/s)
Horizontal speed of the robot's feet in the frames where the ground-truth robot's foot is planted. Real walking gives about 5 cm/s.

2.3 Names of the models

Prompt
A hint given to SAM 3D Body: the 2D position in the image of one of its joints. The model was built so that a person can click such points to fix its output; it then adjusts the whole body to them.
SAM 3D Body
The base model, frozen, frame by frame, with the person's box and the camera given.
SG
Savitzky-Golay filter: a standard smoothing filter that fits a low-order polynomial to each joint's trajectory over a short window (9 frames, order 2). It removes the frame-to-frame shaking.
Oracle prompts
SAM 3D Body given the true image positions of 12 joints as prompts, taken from the motion-capture ground truth. It is not usable in practice (the truth is unknown at test time); it tells how good the output can get if the prompts are perfect.
Prompt corrector
Our network that predicts better prompts (Section 5). 2D-trained is its first training stage alone; with body context is a variant that also reads SAM 3D Body's predicted body over ±2 s around the frame.
Final pipeline
Prompt corrector + SG.
Fixed prompt offset
A baseline without a network: the mean training offset of each key point added to SAM 3D Body's points.
Image-free 3D correction
A baseline network that corrects SAM 3D Body's 3D joints from its own predicted body over ±16 frames, without the image.

3. Data

3.1 NymeriaPlus and what we use from it

Each sequence of Nymeria has video from several Aria glasses (the participant's head and wrists, and an observer who films the participant from a third-person view), audio, narrations written by annotators about every 5 seconds, and the participant's body motion from an XSens suit (inertial sensors on every body segment), 240 times per second, in world coordinates. NymeriaPlus fits that motion to MHR. We use the observer's video as the input, as a stand-in for a third-person camera, and the MHR motion as the ground truth. To compare them we use three more parts of the dataset: the clock synchronisation between the suit and the observer's glasses, the SLAM trajectory of the observer's glasses (the camera moves with the person who films), and the camera calibration.

3.2 Camera and ground truth in the image

The observer records with a fisheye lens, which SAM 3D Body does not expect. We rectify every frame to a 1024 × 1024 pinhole camera with a focal length of 450 px (about 97° field of view), upright, and give the model the exact intrinsics. For every frame, the ground-truth pose is the body frame nearest in time (measured offset below 2.6 ms), moved from the world into the camera with the observer's pose at that instant, and projected with the same intrinsics. On a clip used for the qualitative analysis, shifting the ground truth in time by up to ±2 s against the image evidence gives its best agreement at a shift of 0 frames.

3.3 Selecting the motions

Watching 340 hours of video is not possible, and each video weighs about 16 GB, so the motions are selected from the narrations before any video is downloaded. A narration is a short text with its start and end time, written about every 5 seconds. We kept the motions relevant for a humanoid (sports, dance, exercise, walking, jumping, daily actions) and labelled each narration by the movement the body makes, because the same word covers very different movements ("hit" can be a badminton stroke, a table-tennis stroke or a punch). For dance, badminton, table tennis and soccer the label goes one level deeper: the footwork of a dance, the type of stroke, how a ball is kicked. On 19 sequences the 2465 narrations were labelled by hand (79 actions in 11 categories); on the remaining 185 sequences the labels were assigned by keyword rules written from those hand labels, keeping a fine action only where the rules were precise enough and the category otherwise. The automatic category agrees with the hand labels on 85.6 % of the hand-labelled narrations, an optimistic figure since the rules were written from them; the automatic labels were not checked by hand.

3.4 Cutting clips

A clip is a run of consecutive seconds with the same label, the whole body in the image in every second, a mean limb speed of at least 0.3 m/s and a length of 3–30 s; sitting and idle time are left out. This gives 4428 clips, 628.7 min, 127 people, 11 categories. Each clip is extracted as rectified frames at 30 fps with its ground truth.

3.5 Split by person

Clips of the same person look alike, so training, validation and test contain different people. The 127 people were split so that every category has at least two people in validation and in test (table tennis has only three people with enough minutes, so one per split), preferring held-out people who cover several categories with about 6 minutes in total. 4. SAM 3D Body and where it fails

4.1 SAM 3D Body

SAM 3D Body takes a crop of the person, encodes it with a DINOv3-H+ vision transformer into a 32 × 32 grid of tokens of 1280 channels (the latent: everything the model extracted from the image), and decodes the body with a promptable Transformer decoder that predicts the MHR pose, shape and camera. The decoder can take prompts: 2D positions of any of its 70 key points, or a mask. We use the released DINOv3-H+ checkpoint, frozen, in body mode (without the hand decoders, Appendix A.1), with the box of the person given and the camera intrinsics known.

4.2 Dissection of the model

We measured parameters, compute and time per block on one frame of a dance clip (512 × 512 crop, RTX 4070). Table 2 summarises it.

The encoder holds almost all the parameters and compute, while the decoder takes more time because it is iterative. This shape is what makes our intervention possible: the expensive part runs once per frame and its latent can be stored, and the cheap part can be run again many times with new prompts during training.

The figure below shows the effective receptive field of the encoder token at the position of several joints: the area of the image that holds half of the gradient of that token. For the visible right wrist it covers 2 % of the image around the wrist; for the occluded left wrist it covers 8.6 %, with most of its mass on the head and the torso. When a joint is hidden, the token at its position is built from the context of the body, so its prediction is inferred rather than observed. A principal-component view of the patch features shows region-level structure (person, floor, wall) without a clear separation of body parts at this resolution.

4.3 Baseline on daily-life video

On the 992 hand-labelled clips of 19 people (227 551 frames), SAM 3D Body reaches 85.0 mm MPJPE (57.2 mm PA-MPJPE), and its joints accelerate 4.5 times more than the real motion (26.4 against 5.9 mm/frame²): they shake. The error grows towards the ends of the limbs and changes little across kinds of motion.

Two observations set the direction of the project. First, the error of an occluded joint is a sustained offset, not a spike: only 0.3 % of the joint positions jump by more than 10 cm from one frame to the next, and on the first dance clip the occluded left wrist stays near 211 mm while it is hidden. Second, a classical filter removes the shaking but not that offset (Section 4.4). Whatever we add has to fix sustained errors, not only smooth the trajectory.

4.4 Jitter can be filtered, sustained error cannot

Classical filters act on the output trajectories. On the 992 hand-labelled clips, Savitzky-Golay brings the jitter close to that of the real motion while MPJPE barely moves; One-Euro lags behind the motion. The error of an occluded joint is a sustained offset, not noise, so a model that corrects joints is needed, and a filter is kept only to remove jitter.

4.5 The prompts are a strong lever

The decoder of SAM 3D Body accepts 2D key points as prompts (the keypoint prompts in Figure 6), designed for a person correcting it by hand. If we give it the 12 true key points (shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles), its MPJPE on the 344 test clips falls from 84.2 to 61.4 mm (−27 %), better on every test person. Joints that receive no prompt improve too, because the decoder rearranges the whole body around the points. At test time there are no true key points: the question is how much of that gain a network can recover from what the model already computed.

4.6 Domain shift

NymeriaPlus is not among the data SAM 3D Body was trained or evaluated on. It differs in the camera (a rectified fisheye that moves with the person who films), in the size of the person (10th to 90th percentile of the person's height: 110 to 397 px on the 1024 px image) and in the occlusion of daily life. The SAM 3D Body paper reports 54.8 mm MPJPE on 3DPW, a standard benchmark of outdoor video with motion capture; we measure 84.2 mm here. The two numbers are not strictly comparable: the paper converts its output to SMPL, another common body model, and measures 14 of its joints, we use 18 MHR joints, and 3DPW is part of SAM 3D Body's training data. We did not reproduce the 3DPW number on our setup.

5. Method: the prompt corrector

5.1 Why correct the prompts

The oracle shows that the right 12 prompts remove most of the error, including that of joints without a prompt. Correcting the prompts acts inside the model while keeping it frozen: the final body still comes from SAM 3D Body's decoder and body model, so it stays a valid human body. The prompts must be given for all 12 points in every frame; prompts that appear and disappear between frames make the body jump.

5.2 Architecture

The figure below shows where the corrector sits inside SAM 3D Body (a) and what it is made of (b). For a frame t:

  • Memory: what the model saw. The encoder latent of frames t−1, t and t+1: 3 × 32 × 32 = 3072 tokens of 1280 channels, each projected to 256 channels by a 1 × 1 linear layer. Each token gets the position of its cell in the crop of frame t (16 Fourier frequencies, so a token of a neighbouring frame is placed where it falls in the current crop) and an embedding of which of the three frames it comes from.
  • Queries: where to start. One query per key point. Its content is SAM 3D Body's own prediction of that point: its 2D position in the three frames (normalised to the crop of t) and its 3D position (15 numbers), passed through a small MLP together with Fourier features of the 2D position at t, plus a learned embedding of which joint it is.
  • Network. A Transformer decoder with 2 layers, 8 heads, width 256 and feed-forward width 1024 (pre-normalisation, dropout 0.1). In each layer the 12 queries attend to each other (self-attention: the body is one structure, and a hidden knee can be placed from the hip and ankle) and to the latent tokens (cross-attention: where in the image the evidence for each joint is).
  • Output. For each key point, a 2D correction (in crop units, scaled to pixels) and the logarithm of a scale b, the expected size of the error of that point. The output layer starts at zero, so before training the corrector returns SAM 3D Body's own points; with its own points as prompts the model changes by only +1.0 mm on the first clip, so training starts at the baseline.

The network has 2.54 M parameters, about 0.2 % of SAM 3D Body.

Variant with body context. To give the corrector the motion around the frame, a second version adds SAM 3D Body's whole predicted body (29 joints, 2D in the crop and 3D around the pelvis) for every frame of a longer window, one token per frame (an MLP of the 145 numbers plus a learned embedding of the time offset), to the memory. Frames that fall outside the clip are masked in the attention instead of repeating the end frame. We used windows of ±16 and ±60 frames (±0.5 and ±2 s). Its results are in Table 4 (Section 6.2) and Appendix A.11, and the ±16-frame version in Appendix A.3.

5.3 Why this design

  • Latent instead of the image. The latent is what SAM 3D Body already understood of the image; reading it lets a small network work at the level of body parts instead of pixels, and costs no extra encoder pass.
  • The model's own points as queries. They tell the network where to start, so it only has to learn a correction, which is a smaller and better-conditioned target than an absolute position.
  • Three frames. The neighbouring frames show a joint that is hidden or blurred at t; three frames keep the stored latent per sample small.
  • An uncertainty per point. It comes out of the same loss with no extra label, and it says how much to trust each correction.
  • A correction of the prompts, not of the output. The final body still comes from SAM 3D Body's decoder and body model, so it stays a valid human body.

5.4 Training objectives

Stage 1, in 2D. The corrector is trained to bring its points to the true 2D key points with a Laplace likelihood, which learns the correction and its uncertainty together:

L2D = mean over points of [ ‖ Δ − Δ* ‖1 / b + 2 log b ]

where Δ is the predicted correction, Δ* the correction that would reach the true point, and b the predicted scale.

Stage 2, in 3D through the frozen decoder. Starting from stage 1, every training step runs SAM 3D Body's decoder with the corrected points as prompts (from the stored first-pass state of the frame) and compares the final body with the ground truth: the pelvis-aligned error of 18 body joints, plus 0.1 × L2D. The gradient flows through the frozen decoder into the corrector, so it learns the points that produce the best body, not only the points closest in pixels.

Variant with forced occlusion. To teach the corrector to infer hidden parts, a training variant hides one body part in half of the samples: the latent tokens that fall inside the true 2D box of the lower body, one leg or one arm (plus 15 % margin) are replaced by one learned token in the three frames, and the joints of that part get noise (5 % of the crop in 2D, 5 cm in 3D) in the input points and in the body window. It is used only in training and is evaluated in Appendix A.8.

Variant with body position. The pelvis-aligned error does not see where the body is. The body-context variant adds the 2D reprojection error (in pixels, weight 1) of the 18 joints of the final body, so that the body stays where the person is in the image.

5.5 Implementation

  • Stored latent. The encoder runs once per frame; its latent is stored as 8-bit integers with one scale per channel (1.31 MB per frame instead of 2.6 MB in 16-bit float, with a pose loss of 0.10 mm, Appendix A.9), together with the decoder's first-pass state. For most training clips it is stored only for t−1, t and t+1 around one frame every 30 frames, which keeps the 248 225 training samples within a 789 GB disk.
  • Optimisation. AdamW with cosine decay. Stage 1: 5 epochs, batch 32, learning rate 1e-4, from scratch. Stage 2 and the body-context variant: 2 epochs, batch 16, learning rate 5e-5, starting from stage 1. The model kept is the epoch with the lowest validation MPJPE (stage 1: lowest validation 2D error). Seed 0.
  • Compute. One RTX 4070 (12 GB). One epoch of stage 1 on the final data takes about 27 min; one epoch of stage 2 about 83 min, because every step runs the decoder with gradients (15 ms per frame at batch 16).

6.3 Per person and the position of the body

The 3D-trained corrector improves the pose most but not where the body is: its loss is aligned at the pelvis, so it does not see the body's position (pelvis error 176.8 mm against 136.8 mm for the 2D-trained stage). The body-context variant recovers most of the placement (149.5 mm) at the same pose error.

6.4 Occlusion by objects

On 30 test clips where a table, furniture or another person covers part of the participant (found by inspecting four frames of each clip), the corrector lowers MPJPE from 96.1 to 71.8 mm, slightly below the oracle (73.3 mm): the true 2D key points of a hidden leg are themselves a weak prompt. What it cannot do is shown below: a leg extended back behind the body is hidden in depth, and no 2D prompt can express depth.

In the figure above, SAM 3D Body folds the body forward and down (258 mm in this frame); the corrector brings the trunk near the ground truth (157 mm), and its trunk tilt error over the lean falls from 16.5° to 6.1°. The extended leg, however, is not recovered by any model: in the side view the ground truth has it raised behind the body and every model keeps it bent down. On this clip the 3D-trained corrector reaches 93.6 mm against an upper bound of 93.9 mm, so even the true 2D key points would not place that leg: it is hidden in depth, which a 2D prompt cannot express.

6.5 Training behaviour

The two figures below show the training curves of the final runs. They are short: the stage that runs the decoder in the loop costs about 83 min per epoch on our GPU, so stage 2 was trained for 2 epochs, and the logs keep one value per epoch. This is the weakest part of the study.

In stage 1 the validation error stops improving after three epochs while the training error keeps falling, so the network fits the training people better than it generalises to new ones: more epochs would not help this stage, regularisation would.

In stage 2 the validation error was still falling at the second epoch for the 3D-trained corrector (72.5 → 71.6 mm), so its schedule was cut before it converged. Forced occlusion makes training harder (training error about 5 mm above the variant without it) without changing validation. The validation pelvis error of the body-context variants moves little (193.7 → 191.4 and 189.4 mm) while their training pelvis error rises. One run per model was trained, with no seed variation.

7. Where the gain comes from

The corrector lowers the error from 84.2 to 67.6 mm. Two simpler corrections test what that gain needs. Both use the same people for training, validation and test, and the test set once.

Fixed prompt offset. No network. For each of the 12 key points, the mean of (true 2D point − SAM 3D Body's 2D point) over the 908 278 visible training frames, in units of the crop side (the mean was chosen over the median on validation, 17.7 against 17.8 px). At test time it is added to SAM 3D Body's points, which are then used as prompts exactly like the corrector's. It asks: is the gain a constant difference between the two skeletons?

Image-free 3D correction. A small network (per-frame MLP and a 2-layer Transformer, 1.7 M parameters) that reads SAM 3D Body's predicted body over ±16 frames (29 joints in 2D and 3D, and the pelvis in the camera) and adds a correction to the 18 joints of frame t. No image, no prompts, no decoder. It asks: does the corrector read the image, or does it learn a bias of SAM 3D Body that the body alone reveals?

  • The fixed offset shifts the prompts by a few pixels, mostly downward and largest at the hips and feet (dy +0.7 to +2.6 % of the crop). It lowers MPJPE by 3.6 mm (84.2 → 80.6), better on 189 of the first 223 clips and on every person, and gives almost nothing in PA-MPJPE (55.0 vs 55.1). Most of the gain needs a correction that changes from frame to frame. The same constant shift explains most of the pelvis gain of the 2D-trained stage (220.1 → 152.1 mm, against 136.8).
  • The image-free correction reaches 68.7 mm over the 344 clips, 1.1 mm above the corrector; between people the difference is not distinguishable from zero (bootstrap interval over people [−6.2, +6.0] mm). It is better on the first 223 clips and on the occlusion clips, worse on the 121 new clips (71.1 vs 64.0), and better in PA-MPJPE (41.3 vs 47.5) and smoothness (10.6 vs 18.4 mm/frame²). A control with its own box, computed from SAM 3D Body's joints instead of the ground truth, loses 1.8 mm.

What this means. A large part of the corrector's gain is a systematic bias of SAM 3D Body on this data, which can be corrected from the predicted body alone. Whether the image and the prompts add something on top is not answered: no run combines both. The comparison is also not clean on one variable, since the image-free network reads 33 frames of the body and the corrector 3 frames of the image.

8. From video to the robot

8.1 Retargeting to the Unitree G1

Retargeting turns a human motion into the joint angles of a robot with other proportions. We retargeted 35 test clips to a Unitree G1 humanoid (29 joints) with GMR, an open-source retargeting tool: the body of each model is first converted to SMPL-X (the body model GMR expects) in the world frame (the ground truth comes from NymeriaPlus' own SMPL fit), each predicted motion is filtered with SG before retargeting, and the heading of the first frame is aligned. The clips are the 30 with external occlusion and 6 chosen by the error of SAM 3D Body (one in both). The comparison is kinematic: it does not test whether the robot could execute the motion.

The pose gain mostly does not survive retargeting: the joint angle error falls from 16.7 to 16.0°, joint jitter is the same (1.0 °/frame²), the base trajectory error is about 31–32 cm for both (mostly horizontal: the depth of the predicted pelvis), and the feet slide more. The corrector's pelvis moves more from frame to frame, and the retargeted feet follow it. In a few clips the change is large (below).

We checked the ground truth before using these metrics: the XSens capture drifts in height within some clips (the floor level under the lowest foot varies by a median of 10.4 cm over the 35 clips), but the base trajectory error is almost entirely horizontal (30.6 of 31.2 cm for SAM 3D Body) and does not change when the drift is removed.

8.2 All 35 clips

Choose a clip to compare the four motions beside the real video (same layout as above). Every motion starts at the ground-truth pelvis of the first frame; the floor is at the ground-truth foot height of the first frame. In a few clips the ground-truth robot itself is wrong (for example the lunge, where it leans in the air): the retargeter fails on that input, so the robot metrics of those clips are not reliable.

8.3 Imitation in physics simulation

A retargeted motion is only a reference. To see what a robot does with it, we trained controllers (policies: neural networks that output the robot's joint commands at every step) in the MuJoCo physics simulator, with reinforcement learning (the mjlab motion-tracking task, PPO, 4096 simulated robots in parallel), on one clip: a table-tennis reach where the player leans over the table. The scene includes a table-tennis table of regulation size, placed from the first frame of the video. Three policies, one per reference: SAM 3D Body + SG, prompt corrector + SG, and the ground truth with its height drift removed. Every policy is rewarded for following the reference's body positions, orientations and velocities and the horizontal position of the pelvis; an episode ends when the torso, a wrist or an ankle deviates from the reference by more than 25 cm in height.

First, the references themselves. The video below shows the three motions the policies are asked to imitate, before any physics: in the ground truth one foot always stays on the floor; in the motions estimated from video, both feet leave the floor at the same time, by up to 26 cm for SAM 3D Body and up to 9 cm for the corrector, without any jump in the real clip.

The ground-truth policy reaches full-length episodes within about 1000 iterations; the policies on SAM 3D Body and the corrector need longer, and the SAM 3D Body policy ends with the shortest episodes and the largest error (12 cm, against 7 cm for the corrector and 4 cm for the ground truth).

Both predicted references are physically infeasible: neither keeps a foot on the floor throughout. The trained policies keep a foot down, and they end up as close to each other (4.7 cm, mean body pose difference) as their references were (4.6 cm). They are not closer to the ground-truth motion than their references (SAM 3D Body policy 7.0 cm, reference 7.7 cm; corrector policy 7.6 cm, reference 7.3 cm).

9. Failure cases and limitations

9.1 Failure cases seen in the renders

  • Another person in front. In one walking clip, another person crosses between the camera and the participant for 1.5 s. SAM 3D Body (and so the corrector) reconstructs that person instead: the pelvis jumps from about 4.4 m to 1.3 m from the camera and back, and the robot would jump 3 m. The box of the participant contains both people. SAM 3D Body's paper shows that a mask prompt resolves exactly this case; we did not use it.
  • A distant person and a fast-turning camera. When the person is 5–8 m away, the depth of the pelvis is off by 0.5–0.8 m, and with the camera turning quickly that error moves the body around the world by up to several metres between frames, for both models. The pose itself is stable; the filter, applied in the camera frame, does not remove these jumps.
  • Arms in depth. In a stretching clip the arms project correctly (13–18 px in the image) but point the wrong way in 3D (26–31 cm at the wrists): a 2D prompt cannot fix depth.

9.2 Reliability of the ground truth

In strongly bent poses (picking something up from the floor, leaning over a table or a bed) the projected ground truth does not follow the body in the image in some frames (figure below). We checked the pairing of video and ground truth on one of these clips: the best time offset is 0 frames, the two fits that NymeriaPlus provides from the same suit (MHR and SMPL) agree within about 1 px in the image, and the stored 2D points are the exact projection of the 3D ones. Our extraction therefore reproduces the dataset's body as delivered; the difference comes from the inertial capture or its alignment, which we cannot inspect (the raw suit data are not in the package we use). How many frames are affected was not measured.

This matters for the reading of every result: part of the gain measured in bent poses may be the corrector moving towards the ground truth where the ground truth is less reliable, and part of the remaining error may not be error of the model.

9.3 Limitations

Conditions of the study. The box of the person comes from the ground truth and the camera intrinsics and pose are known. These were chosen to measure the model without detection errors and are part of the scope, so the absolute numbers are lower bounds.

  • One training run per model, with no seed variation; differences of about 1 mm are not established, and bootstrap intervals were computed after the test.
  • Short schedules for the stages that run the decoder (2 epochs); the 3D-trained corrector had not converged.
  • Validation and test have 6 people each; table tennis has one person per split; the occlusion groups are small.
  • The labels of 185 of the 204 sequences are automatic and were not checked by hand.
  • The robot evaluation covers 35 clips chosen mostly for their occlusion; the physics evaluation covers one clip, one rollout per policy, and training stopped at about 37 % of the planned iterations.
  • SAM 3D Body's number on its own benchmark was cited, not reproduced.

10. Conclusion and future work

Used frame by frame on daily-life video, SAM 3D Body makes two kinds of error: jitter, which a classical filter removes, and sustained errors of occluded or misplaced joints, which a filter cannot touch. Rewriting the prompts of its decoder with a small network, followed by the filter, lowers the pose error by 21 % on people it never saw and recovers 77 % of what the true key points would give. A network that never sees the image gets almost the same result, so most of this gain is a bias of the base model on this data rather than information read from the image. At the robot, the gain in pose barely survives retargeting, the feet slide more, and in simulation the policies trained on the two models end up alike, because physics forbids what both references get wrong.

Future work, in order of expected value: combine the image-free correction with the prompt corrector to test whether the image adds anything; a per-joint confidence the robot can use (we tested three signals that need no ground truth: how much the corrected body disagrees with SAM 3D Body's, how much two correctors disagree, and how unstable a joint is over time; they separate joints with more than 10 cm of error from the rest only weakly, and not at all for the feet); filter and correct the motion in the world frame rather than the camera frame; mask prompts when other people are near; more people in training; and reproducing SAM 3D Body on its own benchmark.

Appendix A: ablations

Each ablation changes one thing, and its hypothesis was written before running it, except where noted. Numbers on 223 or 171 test clips come from before the test set was extended.

A.1 Do the hand decoders improve the body?

Question
SAM 3D Body can run extra decoders for the hands. Do they improve the wrists?
Hypothesis
The hand decoders lower the wrist error.
How
Body mode against full mode on the first dance clip (1801 frames).
Result
MPJPE 84.0 against 84.1 mm, left wrist 211.7 in both, 144 against 450 ms per frame. Rejected.
Why
The hand decoders refine the fingers and the hand rotation; the wrist position comes from the arm chain, and the hands are only a few pixels wide in these images. Body mode is the baseline.

The classical filters ablation is in Section 4.4.

A.2 Train in 2D or in 3D through the frozen decoder?

Question
Is it better to train the corrector to reach the true 2D points, or to produce the best 3D body?
Hypothesis
The 3D error improves with the 3D objective, because it optimises the 3D error itself.
How
Same network, same data; stage 1 only against stage 1 + stage 2, on the 223 test clips.
Result
MPJPE: SAM 3D Body 90.6, 2D objective 79.3, 3D objective 71.9 mm. Held. But the pelvis error of the 3D objective is worse (198.6 → 136.3 mm with 2D, 193.3 with 3D), and the 2D error of its points moves back towards the level of SAM 3D Body's own points.
Why
Through the decoder, the network learns the points that make the decoder produce the best pose around the pelvis, not where the joints are. The pelvis-aligned loss does not see where the body is, so it trades the global placement for the pose. The 2D objective is the only one that improves both pose and placement, and its points are closer to the real joints.

A.3 Adding the body position to the loss and the body as context

Question
Can the 3D objective keep the body in place, and does the motion around the frame help?
Hypothesis
The pelvis error improves, the MPJPE goes down, and a ±16-frame window of the predicted body reduces the jitter.
How
Three changes run together (a decision of the team, accepting that their effects cannot be separated): a reprojection term in the loss, SAM 3D Body's predicted body as input, over ±16 frames.
Result
On the 223 test clips: pelvis 193.3 → 146.5 mm (held); MPJPE 71.9 → 72.6 mm (not held); acceleration error 18.9 → 19.2 mm/f² (not held).
Why
The reprojection term keeps the body where it is in the image at a small cost in pose. The window does not bring smoothness: each frame is still corrected on its own.

A.4 Filtering after the corrector

Question
Does the filter that removes SAM 3D Body's jitter also remove the jitter the corrector adds, without losing its gain?
Hypothesis
The acceleration error of every corrector falls at least 70 % and ends below the unfiltered baseline; MPJPE changes by less than 2 %; the best corrector keeps at least a 15 % reduction against the filtered baseline; the pelvis error changes by less than 5 %.
How
Savitzky-Golay (window 9, order 2) on the 3D joints of every model, 171 test clips.
Result
Held on the four points: acceleration error 20.0 → 4.0 (−80 %), MPJPE −1.4 %, 70.4 against 88.3 mm for the filtered baseline (−20.3 %), pelvis −1 %.
Why
The jitter is high-frequency and the corrections are low-frequency, so the filter separates them. The filter does not repair the pelvis bias of the 3D objective.

A.5 More frames of the same people

Question
Does more data of the same people help?
Hypothesis
The MPJPE falls by less than 2 mm.
How
Training clips from 685 to 1038 (+58 % frames), same people and categories, same recipe, evaluated on 171 test clips.
Result
71.4 → 70.1 mm (−1.3; 95 % interval over clips [−1.6, −0.8], computed after the test); better on 5 of 6 people. Held.
Why
More frames of the same people repeat what the network already saw; the gain is small but consistent.

A.6 More people

Question
Does adding new people help more than adding frames?
Hypothesis
The MPJPE falls by less than 2 mm on the same test clips; on the new test clips the gain over SAM 3D Body is at least 15 %.
How
2906 more training and validation clips, of which the training ones add 50 new people and +10 % samples; same recipe, same 223 test clips.
Result
71.9 → 70.3 mm (−1.6; 95 % interval over clips [−1.9, −1.3], over people [−2.2, −1.1], computed after the test); better on 174 of 223 clips and on 6 of 6 people. On the 121 new test clips −15.5 % against SAM 3D Body. Held.
Why
With +10 % samples the new people give more than +58 % frames of the same people did: variety of people matters more than quantity. On the new test clips the gain over the previous model cannot be told from zero, so the benefit is on the people already represented.

A.7 A longer context window, with masking

Question
Can a window of ±2 s of the predicted body recover a limb that stays hidden for longer than ±0.5 s?
Hypothesis
The pelvis error of the body-context variant improves over the ±16-frame version; the trunk tilt error on the table-tennis clip does not get worse.
How
Window ±60 frames, frames outside the clip masked in the attention (52 % of the training frames have a window that crosses a clip end). It was trained together with the additional people of A.6, so the window and the data change at once.
Result
Pelvis 146.5 → 145.3 mm (held, barely); MPJPE 72.6 → 71.1; trunk tilt error on the lean 5.8° → 6.4° (not held). On the occlusion clips it is worse than the 3D-trained corrector (74.9 against 71.8 mm).
Why
The window only adds the predicted body, not the image of the other frames; if SAM 3D Body is wrong about a limb during the whole occlusion, the window repeats that error. Its cost in memory is small (from 33 to 121 tokens next to 3072 latent tokens).

A.8 Forced occlusion during training

Question
Does hiding body parts during training teach the corrector to infer them?
Hypothesis
On the 15 "table over the legs" clips the forced occlusion lowers MPJPE by at least 2 mm, and by at least 1 mm on the 30 occlusion clips; on the 223 clips the two variants stay within 1 mm.
How
With probability 0.5 per sample, the latent tokens inside the true 2D box of the lower body, one leg or one arm (plus 15 % margin) are replaced by one learned token in t−1, t and t+1, and the joints of that part get noise (5 % of the crop in 2D, 5 cm in 3D) in the body window and in the input points. Everything else equal to A.7.
Result
−0.8 mm on the 15 clips (interval [−1.7, −0.2]), +0.1 mm on the 30, +0.6 mm on the 223. Not held. The acceleration error fell by about 1 mm/f², which was not predicted.
Why
Masking the latent removes evidence but keeps SAM 3D Body's first pass, which was computed from the clean image, so the occlusion is not the one the model meets at test time. Discarded.

A.9 How the latent is stored

Question
How much can the stored latent be compressed without changing what the decoder produces?
Hypothesis
A storage format is acceptable if the decoder's pose moves less than 1 mm.
How
The first-pass decoder on the stored latent against the full latent, every fourth frame of two test people.
Result
PCA to 256 / 384 / 512 / 768 channels: 12.0 / 5.8 / 3.4 / 1.7 mm. 8-bit float: 0.27–0.55 mm. 8-bit integer per channel: 0.10 mm (1.31 MB per frame). 6 / 5 / 4 bits: 0.52 / 1.08 / 2.36 mm.
Why
The channels of the latent have very different ranges, so one scale per channel keeps them; reducing the number of channels discards information the decoder uses. 8-bit integers were kept.

A.10 A pretrained motion prior

Question
Can a generative model of human motion fill a limb that is hidden, from the motion of the rest of the body?
Expected
If the prior recovers the leg of the table-tennis clip when we tell it which joints are hidden, the idea is worth training on our data; if it does not even reproduce the visible motion, this prior cannot be used. (Written before the run, not before the idea.)
How
The released MDM trained on HumanML3D, frozen, with DNO: its input noise is optimised so that the generated motion matches an observation. Three costs on the table-tennis clip, all starting from the motion of SAM 3D Body: (control) all 22 joints of SAM 3D Body in 3D; (1) only the trusted joints in 3D, with the hidden ones taken from the ground-truth occlusion mask and the legs behind the table marked by hand (an upper bound); (2) no ground truth: the 12 corrected 2D points, each weighted by 1/b, projected with the known camera, so that depth is left to the prior. In MDM's skeleton at 20 fps, so the numbers are not comparable with the rest of the report. We also asked MDM to reproduce the real motion itself.
Result
SAM 3D Body 111.1 mm (legs 195.4). Control 162.3, (1) 173.2, (2) 184.9 mm; legs 251–297 mm. Reproducing the real motion: 142.2 mm after 300 optimisation steps and 72.7 mm after 1000.
Why
This prior cannot represent the motion of the clip: it reproduces the real motion with more error than SAM 3D Body estimates it, so filling the hidden leg on top of it only adds error. The test says nothing about priors trained on this kind of motion, which is left as future work (Section 10).

A.11 The body-context variant on the full test set

Question
Does giving the corrector SAM 3D Body's predicted body over ±2 s (one token per frame, frames outside the clip masked) improve the pose, the position of the body, or what reaches the robot?
How
Same training recipe as the prompt corrector (2 epochs in 3D from the 2D-trained stage), with the 2D reprojection term in the loss; also a version with forced occlusion (A.8). Evaluated once on the 344 test clips, on the 30 occlusion clips and, after SG, on the 35 retargeted clips.
Result
Pose: 67.8 mm against 67.6 (with SG 66.9 against 66.7). Position: pelvis error 149.5 mm against 176.8 (−15 %). Occlusion clips: 74.9 against 71.8 mm, pelvis 252.1 against 365.8 mm. Robot: joint angle error 15.6° against 16.0°, base trajectory 28.6 against 32.4 cm, foot sliding 57.6 against 68.0 cm/s.
Why
The window carries SAM 3D Body's predicted bodies, not the images of the other frames, so it cannot recover a limb SAM 3D Body gets wrong for the whole window; the reprojection term keeps the body where it is in the image, which is what improves the pelvis and, through it, the base trajectory and the feet of the robot.
