How to read this book

This Research Decision Book records every design decision with its justification, the alternatives we discarded, and where an AI assistant was used. Each ablation has its hypothesis written before running it, followed by the result and whether the hypothesis held.

AI assistance. We used Claude Code (Anthropic) as a coding agent. Every entry says what it did (AI); the decision itself and its interpretation are the team's, and ablation hypotheses are written by the team, not by the assistant.

Labels: decisions are D1, D2, … in the order they appear in this story; experiments already run are E1, E2, …; planned ablations are A1, A2, …. Figures and tables are numbered and referenced from the text.

Short context

The main problem is that motion capture is too expensive to collect enough data to feed the imitation-learning loop of humanoid robots. Because of that, one option is to use videos taken with cameras. There are two options: cameras mounted in a specific laboratory area, or videos of daily routines, with camera movement, occlusion, etc.

This project covers the second one. We work with the Nymeria dataset[3], which has videos of daily routines with occlusion: 1 100 sequences (340 h) in 20 scenarios, such as cooking, housekeeping, workout, dance, hiking or playing games with a partner.

Given this dataset, the question is how to extract the trajectory of each human joint in order to build the trajectory of the whole body. For this project we use SAM 3D Body[1]. Because of occlusion, frame-by-frame processing and camera movement, the main research question is whether SAM 3D Body, a single-image model, can be trusted on video, and how to correct it where it fails. Specifically we ask: which trajectories are not good? How can we improve them?

Sections

This book follows the process we have followed since the start of the project. Stages 1 and 2 answer: which data do we use? Stage 3 answers: which model, and where do we change it? The last part answers directly the tasks of the course project document: Phase A (teardown), the experiments, the ablations and Phase C.

1) Study and decide on the Nymeria dataset

For each sequence, Nymeria provides video from several Aria glasses (the participant's head and wrists, and an observer who records the participant from a third-person view), audio, a narration (text that describes what the participant does), and the participant's body motion, captured with an XSens suit.

SAM 3D Body outputs the body in a specific skeleton model, MHR (Momentum Human Rig[2]: Meta's parametric body model, 127 joints). We decided to use NymeriaPlus[4] because its motion is already in that skeleton.

Explore the Nymeria dataset

D1 · Challenge data: NymeriaPlus instead of Nymeria 2026-10-17

  • Decision. Use NymeriaPlus: the observer camera (fisheye Aria glasses) and the XSens ground truth.
  • Why. NymeriaPlus provides the ground-truth motion already fitted to MHR (127 joints), the same skeleton that SAM 3D Body outputs, so no skeleton mapping is needed. Nymeria is also outside SAM 3D Body's training and evaluation data (3DPW, AI Challenger, COCO, EgoExo4D, EgoHumans, Harmony4D, MPII, SA-1B), and its fisheye lens, moving camera and distant or occluded people make it a good domain shift (Phase C).
  • Alternatives. The original Nymeria (XSens skeleton only): it would need a mapping between the XSens and MHR joints.
  • AI. Helped us analyse and understand the tools of Nymeria and SAM 3D Body.

Nymeria is not a dataset made for humanoid robots, so we have to explore its content and filter the relevant data, specifically the videos where the observer records the participant. To find them we use the narrations (below).

Evaluate the quality of the videos

We explored the quality of the videos, mainly their limitations: camera movement, occlusion, and situations we are not interested in, such as a person sitting. We concluded that occlusion and camera movement will be the most interesting challenges. A second challenge is the camera itself: the observer records with a fisheye lens, which is uncommon in the video pipelines used for imitation learning. This leads to the next decision.

D2 · Undistortion to a fixed pinhole camera 2026-10-17

  • Decision. Rectify the fisheye image to a 1024×1024 pinhole (f = 450 px, ~97° field of view), upright, and pass the exact intrinsics to SAM 3D Body.
  • Why. SAM 3D Body expects a perspective camera; giving it the true camera matrix removes the error of estimating the field of view.
  • Limitation. A wide field of view stretches people near the border of the image. A virtual camera that points at the person is planned as ablation A6 (Phase C).
  • AI. Wrote the rectification with Meta's projectaria_tools (fisheye calibration from the recording → pinhole calibration); output size and focal length chosen with the assistant.

Evaluate additional relevant information

Each sequence has a narration and the XSens motion. A narration is a short text written by annotators, with its start and end time, about every 5 seconds (e.g. "C is running in the backyard and then turns around"). We use them because watching 340 hours of video is not possible and each video weighs ~16 GB, while the narrations are only text: searching them tells us which sequences, and which minutes of each one, contain whole-body motion before downloading any video.

The XSens motion comes from a suit with inertial sensors (accelerometers and gyroscopes); it uses no camera. It gives the 3D pose of the whole body 240 times per second, in world coordinates, and NymeriaPlus fits it to MHR. Since it is the true trajectory of the motion, we use it as ground truth and for supervised learning. To compare it with SAM 3D Body, which predicts the pose as seen from the camera, we need three more pieces: the clock synchronisation between the suit and the observer's glasses (to pair each frame with the pose at that instant), the observer's SLAM trajectory (the glasses' own position and orientation, to bring the pose from the world into the moving camera), and the camera calibration (to project the joints onto the image).

Figure 1 checks that these three pieces fit together: the ground-truth skeleton, synchronised in time and moved into the camera with the SLAM pose, is drawn on the rectified image of D2. If any of them were wrong, the skeleton would drift from the body or lag behind the motion; in the excerpt it stays on the person while she moves.

2) Select and extract classes

Read the video narrations of the dataset

We read the narrations to find where the movements happen. We counted movement keywords (jump, kick, dance, run, …) in the 844 narrated sequences and chose 19 sequences with whole-body motion, from five scenarios: Workout, Dance, Game night, Do as I command and Fresh air.

Select the classes to study

The narrations tell us where to look, but not whether the body is actually visible. For that, without the video, we draw the ground-truth skeleton as the observer camera sees it (a preview) and measure, every second, whether the whole body is inside the image, how many joints are hidden by the body itself, and how fast the limbs move.

Figure 2 is a preview of one dance sequence: green joints are visible from the camera, red ones are hidden behind the body, and the narration of that moment appears below. Figure 3 summarises the whole 14-minute sequence second by second: the whole body is in the image 93 % of the time (top), between 5 % and 30 % of the joints are self-occluded most of the time (middle), and the limbs move at 0.3–0.5 m/s except in a few pauses (bottom). Those pauses and the gaps in the top row are the seconds a clip must avoid.

With these previews we take three decisions about imperfect data: we keep occlusion, because it is the challenge we want to study; we discard the seconds where the body is not fully inside the image, because there is no complete body to compare against; and we note that the occlusion we measure is only self-occlusion, since the preview has no image, so furniture and other people are not counted.

D3 · Short clips, one movement each 2026-10-19

  • Decision. Evaluate on short clips, each one a single movement, cut from the previews.
  • Why. Evaluating per movement type tells us whether an effect generalises across motions (dancing is not kicking a ball), and short clips can be processed one by one.
  • First clip. dance_salsa_01 (S6-Dance, 60 s) was chosen by hand from the previews to run the baseline (E1–E3).
  • AI. Wrote the preview renderer; the team reviewed the previews and picked the first clip.

D4 · Labels that describe the movement of the body 2026-10-20

  • Why. The same word in a narration covers very different movements: "hit" can be a badminton stroke, a table-tennis stroke or a punch; "jump" can be a real jump or mimicking a jump rope; "dance" can be a square step, a cross step or a turn.
  • Decision. Read every narration in usable time (2 465) and label it by the movement it describes. For dance, badminton, table tennis and soccer (1 468 narrations) one level further: the footwork of the dance (dance_square_step, dance_cross_step), the stroke (badminton_overhead_stroke, badminton_serve, table_tennis_reach_stroke) and how the ball is kicked (soccer_receive_and_pass, soccer_run_and_kick). Result: 79 actions in 11 macro categories.
  • AI. Read and labelled the narrations; the team set the level of detail and approved the macro categories.

D5 · Clip library: every usable window of the 19 sequences 2026-10-20

  • Decision. A clip is a run of consecutive seconds with the same label (D4), the whole body in the image in every second, mean limb speed ≥ 0.3 m/s and 3–30 s (longer runs split evenly); idle and sitting excluded.
  • Why. As much data as the 19 sequences give, for every movement type, each clip exactly one action. Not used: 44 min too slow, 17 min in runs shorter than 3 s.
  • AI. Built the clips from the labels; the team set the criteria.

Table 1 shows the result: 992 clips (126.5 min) of 79 actions. Soccer and dance dominate in minutes; jump and stretch are rare.

Not every use of the data needs the images: to learn how a body moves, the motion capture alone is enough; only to see where SAM 3D Body fails do we need the video, and the video is what does not fit on our disk. So we keep two kinds of data:

D6 · Two kinds of data: motion without video, images only where SAM 3D Body runs 2026-10-19

  • Decision. Motion capture of the whole 19 sequences without video (308 min, ~45 MB per sequence), to pre-train with simulated occlusion; and clips with images only where SAM 3D Body has to run. Each video is downloaded, its clips are cut, and the video is deleted, one sequence at a time.
  • Why. Images weigh ~0.3 GB per minute of clip at 30 fps; motion capture is light and covers every minute.
  • AI. Wrote the download and extraction scripts, with a free-disk check.

3) Inference with SAM 3D Body

Study the model

SAM 3D Body estimates the 3D body of one person from one image: an encoder (DINOv3[5]) reads the crop around the person, and a promptable decoder predicts the MHR parameters. It needs a box around the person and, optionally, the camera intrinsics; it also accepts 2D key points as prompts, like the SAM family.

D7 · Base model: SAM 3D Body, DINOv3-H+ checkpoint, frozen 2026-10-17

  • Decision. Use the released DINOv3-H+ checkpoint (840 M encoder) and never fine-tune it.
  • Why. It is the better of the two released checkpoints, and at ~1 B parameters it cannot be trained on our 12 GB GPU. Frozen inference also gives a clean baseline.
  • Alternatives. ViT-H checkpoint (631 M): slightly worse in the paper.
  • AI. Environment setup and checkpoint download.

D8 · Box from the ground truth for now 2026-10-17

  • Decision. The box around the person comes from the projected ground-truth joints, not from a detector.
  • Why. The repository's detector (ViTDet-H) and SAM 3D Body do not fit together in 12 GB; first we measure the model without detection errors.
  • Consequence. The reported errors are a lower bound; a real detector is ablation A7.

Test the model following the repository

We first ran the model as the repository shows (one image), and then on our first clip, dance_salsa_01, in its two modes: only the body, or the body plus the hand decoders (E1, E2). Table 2 compares them.

D9 · body inference mode as the baseline 2026-10-17

  • Decision. Use the body decoder only (body mode), not the body + hand decoders (full).
  • Why. On dance_salsa_01 the hand decoders do not improve the body and triple the time per frame (Table 2).

D10 · Compute: GPU jobs on Kaggle, data processing local 2026-10-19 · dropped 2026-10-21 (D17)

  • Decision (first). Heavy GPU runs on Kaggle (2× T4) through its API; downloads and preprocessing local.

Run the teardown

The teardown (Phase A, below) shows where the model spends its parameters and time, and that the prediction of an occluded joint is built from the context of the body, not from what is visible at its position. The baselines (E1–E3) show that SAM 3D Body jitters, with acceleration 4.4× the ground truth's; a simple filter removes that jitter, but not the error of the occluded wrist, which stays wrong for as long as it is occluded. This tells us where to intervene:

D11 · Where to intervene: inside the model, on the decoder pose tokens 2026-10-19 · replaced 2026-10-21 (D17)

  • Decision (first). Temporal attention over the decoder's pose tokens across T frames, before the MHR head, with the rest of the model frozen.
  • Why. A filter after the model is post-processing; the course asks for a change in the model's core.
  • Replaced. The intervention became P3b, the correction of the decoder's prompts (D14, D17).

E1–E3 are one clip. Before extracting the library (D5), we wanted to fix the whole pipeline (inference, evaluation, failure atlas, correction) on clips we already had with images.

D12 · First study on the 67 local clips of catalog_v_old 2026-10-20

To study occlusion per joint and per frame on these clips, we need to know which joints are hidden in each frame:

D13 · Per-joint self-occlusion from the participant's real body mesh 2026-10-20

  • Decision. For every frame, a joint is self-occluded if the participant's body surface is more than 12 cm in front of it as seen from the camera (z-buffer of the mesh on 8 px cells of the 1024 image), the same rule as the previews. The surface is the participant's real MHR mesh, nearest body frame.
  • Why. Without the body files the mesh would need a mean body shape, and joints ~10 cm under the skin (pelvis, neck, hips) would be marked hidden too often. With the real mesh, dance_salsa_01 gives 16.1 % of hidden joints, as its preview (per-frame correlation 0.88). The other 66 clips were not checked against a preview or by eye.
  • Limitation. Only self-occlusion: furniture, objects and other people are not counted, so it is a lower bound. Measuring them (e.g. with a person mask) was discarded: it would not change any decision of the plan.
  • AI. Wrote the script and downloaded the body files of the 10 sequences (deleted after use).

Figure 4 shows what D13 measures over the 67 clips. Left: the hips are self-occluded in 31 % of the frames and the shoulders in 23–25 %, while knees and ankles are hidden in less than 10 %. The torso or an arm usually stands between the camera and the hips. Right: the number of the 12 key joints hidden in a frame is usually 1 to 3 (mean 2.17); 16 % of the frames have none, and no frame has more than 8.

With the oracle E4 (Experiments) we measured how much better 2D key points can help SAM 3D Body through its own promptable decoder: the 12 true key points lower MPJPE by 27 %, and most of the gain comes from the visible joints. At test time there are no true key points, so the question becomes how much of that gain a network can recover:

D14 · P3b: a small network that corrects SAM 3D Body's 12 key points, used as its prompts 2026-10-20

  • Decision. SAM 3D Body stays frozen. For frame \(t\), a small network reads SAM 3D Body's encoder latent and its predicted 12 key points (2D and 3D) for the frames \(t-1, t, t+1\), and outputs a 2D correction of each key point and its confidence. The corrected points go back into SAM 3D Body's decoder as prompts.
    • Network: a Transformer decoder in the style of DETR[8]: 12 learned joint queries attend to the \(3\times32\times32\) latent tokens (after a 1×1 projection 1280 → 256) and to each other; 2 layers, 2.5 M parameters. Its output layer starts at zero, so at first it returns SAM 3D Body's own points.
    • Loss: first in 2D, a Laplace likelihood \(\lVert\hat\delta-\delta\rVert_1/b + 2\log b\), so the confidence \(b\) comes out of the same loss[9]; then fine-tuned in 3D through the frozen decoder (final body vs ground truth, 18 joints, pelvis-aligned).
    • Prompts: always the 12 key joints; "only those above a confidence threshold" is kept as an alternative.
  • Why. The prompt encoder only accepts 2D points; E4 shows the 12 key ones lower the error a lot. We cannot know at test time which joints are occluded, and E4 (a) shows that prompting only the occluded ones recovers little and doubles the jitter. The latent is what SAM 3D Body already understood of the image, so the network can be small. Correction 0 is almost the baseline: SAM 3D Body with its own 12 points as prompts changes MPJPE by +1.0 mm on dance_salsa_01 (84.0 → 85.0).
  • Alternatives (kept as ablations). The image crop instead of the latent; without the predicted points; only frame \(t\); a causal window; absolute positions instead of a correction; more predicted key points than the 12 (face, fingers and toes judged not relevant); only 2D or only 3D loss.
  • AI. Proposed the options; the team chose each part.

D16 · Store the full encoder latent of the 67 clips 2026-10-21

  • Decision. Save SAM 3D Body's encoder latent of every frame once (32×32×1280, float16, ~2.6 MB per frame): 50 GB. Each clip checks that the pose of this pass equals the saved baseline (max difference 0.003 mm).
  • Why. Training reads the latent at every pass; running the encoder each time would take ~45 min per pass.
  • Open issue. For the whole library the latent would be ~600 GB at 30 fps. Before that, we will measure how much is lost by compressing it (e.g. 1280 → 256 channels with PCA) by running the decoder on the reconstructed latent.

D17 · The intervention is P3b only; everything runs locally 2026-10-21

  • Decision. The intervention is P3b (D14), trained first in 2D (E5) and then in 3D through the frozen decoder (E6). The temporal attention of D11 is not done, and the Kaggle route of D10 is dropped.
  • Why. E4 shows that the decoder's prompts are a strong lever (−27 % MPJPE with the true points), and P3b acts inside the model through that interface with SAM 3D Body frozen. The local GPU was enough for every run so far.
  • Consequence. Ablations A1–A3 and A5 were written for D11; A8–A13 replace them.

Figure 7 is the effective receptive field of the encoder token at the patch of five joints: the area of the image whose pixels hold 50 % of the gradient of that token. For the visible right wrist it is a tight patch (2 % of the image) around the wrist. For the occluded left wrist it is wider (8.6 %) and its mass sits on the head and torso, not on the wrist: when a joint is not visible, the encoder builds its token from the context of the body, so its prediction is inferred, not observed. This is the first clue of the sustained wrist error found in E1 and E3.

This is why, for Figure 5, we removed the part each grid cell has in common over many frames before looking at the latent: what is left does follow the person.

Course tasks · Experiments

Common setup: frozen SAM 3D Body, box from the ground truth, known intrinsics, 18 body joints of the MHR skeleton, frames with the whole body in view. Metrics: MPJPE after pelvis alignment, PA-MPJPE (after aligning rotation, scale and position), acceleration error (mm/frame²).

E1 · Baseline on dance_salsa_01, body mode 2026-10-17

MPJPE 84.0 mm, PA-MPJPE 57.0 mm, and acceleration 16.4 vs 3.7 mm/frame² for the ground truth: 4.4× jitter. Worst joints: left wrist 212 mm, right wrist 127 mm, head 119 mm. The left arm is occluded at times.

Figure 9 shows the 10 s of the clip where the left wrist is worst (268 mm on average; the wrist is self-occluded 47 % of that time). The red skeleton (SAM 3D Body) stays on the person and close to the green one (ground truth) for the legs and torso, while the arms differ more; over these 10 s the left wrist is on average 268 mm from its true position. Figure 10 shows the whole clip: the error (top) varies between ~60 and ~120 mm without settling, and the acceleration of SAM 3D Body (red, bottom) stays well above the ground truth's (green) all the time, with one spike near the end.

E2 · Full mode (hand decoders) 2026-10-17

  • Hypothesis (written before): the hand decoders lower the wrist error.
  • Result: no change (PA-MPJPE 57.1 mm, left wrist 211.7 mm, Table 2). Rejected. The hand decoder refines the fingers and the rotation of the hand; the wrist position comes from the arm chain, and the hands are only a few pixels wide here.

E4 · Ground-truth 2D key points as prompts (oracle) on catalog_v_old 2026-10-20

  • Setup. The 67 clips (20 165 frames, 13 categories), body mode, same box and intrinsics as E1. The body decoder is run a second time with 2D key-point prompts taken from the ground truth: (a) only the key joints self-occluded in the frame (D13), or (b) always the 12 key joints.
  • Hypothesis (team, written before running): giving SAM 3D Body the ground-truth 2D key points as prompts will lower the error, because they are the true positions; this is an upper bound. The interesting question is how much of that gain a small predictor, trained with the ground truth on our frames, can recover.

Table 7 gives the totals. (b) lowers all three metrics by about a quarter. (a), with 2.17 prompts per frame on average, lowers MPJPE by only 6.5 % and doubles the acceleration error.

Figure 12 breaks MPJPE down by category. (b) (blue) is the lowest in every category, by 11 to 33 mm; (a) (orange) sits close to the baseline (grey), and in walk backward it does not change it at all. Figure 13 breaks it down by joint: (b) also lowers joints that receive no prompt, such as the head (−30 mm) and the toes (−24 and −32 mm), because fixing the shoulders and hips moves the whole skeleton.   • Held? Yes for (b). Beyond the hypothesis: the gain needs all 12 joints in every frame, not only the occluded ones.
  • Limitation. Only self-occlusion is measured (D13).

E5 · P3b, 2D loss only 2026-10-21

  • Setup. The D14 network trained with the 2D loss on the training people of D15 (49 clips, 16 107 evaluated frames), validated on steven (6 clips, 900 frames); the test people are not used. AdamW, lr 1e-4 with cosine decay, batch 32, 20 epochs (~47 s each); the model kept is the one with the lowest validation 2D error.
  • Hypothesis (team): to fill in (this first run fixes the pipeline; the hypothesis was not written before it).

Figure 14 is the training curve. On the validation person, the mean 2D error of the 12 points drops from 11.0 px (SAM 3D Body alone, dashed) to 4.6 px at epoch 6, and stays at ~4.7 px afterwards, while the training error keeps falling to 4.2 px: from epoch 6 on, the network is learning the training people rather than the task.

E6 · P3b, 3D fine-tuning through the frozen decoder 2026-10-21

  • Setup. Starts from the E5 model. Each training step runs SAM 3D Body's frozen decoder with the network's points as prompts, from the stored first-pass state of the frame and the stored latent; loss = MPJPE of the 18 joints after pelvis alignment (mm) + 0.1 × the 2D loss of E5. Points outside the crop are not used, as in E5. All training frames every epoch, AdamW lr 5e-5 with cosine decay, batch 16, 10 epochs (~5 min each); the model kept is the one with the lowest validation MPJPE. Measured before: 15 ms per frame at batch 16 and 1.8 GB of GPU memory; the decoder run from the stored latent gives the saved poses (≤ 0.001 mm).
  • Hypothesis (team, written before the result): we expect the 3D error to improve over E5, because now what is optimised is the 3D error itself.
